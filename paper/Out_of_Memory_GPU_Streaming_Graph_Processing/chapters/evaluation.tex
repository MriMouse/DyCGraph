\subsection{Experiment Setup}
% 实验环境
\subsubsection{Experimental Environment} We implement our system in C++ and CUDA and evaluate it on a server with two Intel Xeon Gold 5218R processors (40 physical cores and 80 hardware threads in total) and an NVIDIA Tesla V100 GPU. Each CPU has a base frequency of 2.10 GHz. The V100 has 80 streaming multiprocessors (5,120 CUDA cores) and 16 GB of global memory. The GPU connects to the host via PCIe 3.0 $\times$16, with a theoretical peak bandwidth of approximately 16 GB/s per direction. We compile the code with NVIDIA NVCC 12.1 and GCC 12.4.0 using the \texttt{-O3} optimization flag.  % 这块软硬件环境，后面需要和绍炎核对一下。
% 数据集
\subsubsection{Datasets} % 数据集都是从哪下载的，需要引用一下地址
We evaluate CoIncGraph on six real-world graphs whose statistics are summarized in Table~\ref{tab:datasets}. OK~\cite{OKFS}, WK~\cite{WK}, TW~\cite{TW}, and FS~\cite{OKFS} are social networks with power-law degree distributions, whereas EU and USA are road networks with large diameters. Following Grapin and GraphBolt~\cite{2025-Grapin,2019-GraphBolt}, we uniformly sample edges without replacement and assign half to insertions and half to deletions. We remove the insertion edges from the original graph to form the initial graph $G_0$, then randomly interleave the updates and partition them into ten equal-sized batches, each containing equal numbers of insertions and deletions.
% Table~\ref{tab:datasets} presents the six real-world graph datasets used in our study. OK, WK, TW, and FS are social networks—characterized by power-law distributions—while EU and USA are road networks, which possess larger diameters. Our streaming workload construction follows the approach used in [Grapin, Graphbolt]. First, we perform uniform sampling of edges from the original graph without replacement; half of the sampled edges are designated for insertion and the other half for deletion. The edges designated for insertion are excluded from the original graph to form the initial graph, $G_0$. The resulting update operations are randomly interleaved and partitioned into ten consecutive batches, with each batch containing an equal number of insertion and deletion operations. This method effectively preserves the edge distribution characteristics of the datasets.

% 对比基线
\subsubsection{Baselines} We compare CoIncGraph with three state-of-the-art systems: SHGraph, Ingress, and Grapin. SHGraph~\cite{2020-SHGraph}, Gunrock's dynamic graph module, uses per-vertex Slab Hash tables to support parallel graph updates and queries in GPU memory. Ingress~\cite{2021-Ingress} performs CPU-based incremental graph processing and automatically selects a memoization policy (memoization-free, memoization-path, memoization-vertex, or memoization-edge) according to the algorithm properties, so that only the memoized states required by the algorithm are maintained and revised. Grapin~\cite{2025-Grapin} supports CPU--GPU cooperative out-of-memory (OOM) streaming graph processing by maintaining graph topology in host memory and executing incremental computation on the GPU. It combines GPU hot-subgraph caching with optimized zero-copy access to reduce CPU--GPU data movement.
% 后面可以考虑，看要不要评估方法的简单说明，大概一段
\subsubsection{Methodology} We evaluate all systems on SSSP, BFS, CC, and PR using identical initial graphs and update sequences. We measure end-to-end processing time and update throughput (processed update edges per second), including graph updates, incremental computation, and CPU--GPU transfers where applicable, but excluding initial graph loading and preprocessing. Speedup is the ratio of baseline processing time to that of CoIncGraph. We report the arithmetic mean of five independent runs, each starting from the same initial graph. We also examine sensitivity to [batch size and other parameters] and quantify individual optimizations through ablation studies.

\textbf{Exp1: Comparison with CPU-based systems.} CoIncGraph outperforms Ingress in all 48 cases, achieving speedups of 4.2$\times$--1979.0$\times$ through GPU parallelism and compact incremental computation. Although Ingress restricts computation to affected states through memoization, its performance is constrained by CPU parallelism and memory bandwidth. The gains are largest for BFS and SSSP, followed by CC (124.5$\times$): Ingress maintains path or vertex dependencies for these algorithms, and repairing invalidated results requires multiple traversal rounds over the host-resident graph. Gains are smaller for PR, where Ingress uses memoization-free, delta-based accumulation to propagate update-induced changes without maintaining dependencies. CoIncGraph also benefits from larger graphs, achieving geometric-mean speedups of 104.5$\times$ on the two smaller graphs and 156.2$\times$ on the two billion-scale graphs, whose larger affected regions expose more GPU parallelism.

\textbf{Exp5: Effect of source-isolated graph organization.} Disabling source isolation increases execution time by a factor of 2.42--8.26. In the PMA, edge updates may shift elements and trigger rebalancing, relocating adjacency lists of unchanged sources and requiring their descriptors to be republished. As batch size increases, CoIncGraph's maintenance cost grows with the number of changed sources, whereas the shared array remains dominated by unrelated adjacency movement, narrowing the performance gap.

\textbf{Exp6: Effect of unified change view.} CoIncGraph-NoView is 2.62$\times$--9.81$\times$ slower than CoIncGraph. Without changed-source lists, it reloads all GPU vertex descriptors each batch and invalidates cached adjacency of updated sources rather than repairing it. In contrast, CoIncGraph publishes one descriptor per changed source and selectively repairs affected cache entries, limiting maintenance to the changes in each batch. The average slowdown is larger on FS, reaching 4.77$\times$, partly because FS has 25\% more vertices than TW and full descriptor publication scales with the vertex count.

\textbf{Exp7: Effect of compact incremental computation.} Disabling compact computation increases execution time by a factor of 2.72--9.06. Despite the small number of invalidated vertices per batch, CoIncGraph-NoCompact scans all vertices each round to identify them and defers their repair to insertion processing. It then reactivates all vertices and rebuilds graph-wide worklists, incurring full-graph overhead regardless of batch size. By completing deletion repair before insertion processing, CoIncGraph initiates insertion propagation only from destinations improved by inserted edges and builds worklists directly from successful updates, restricting computation to affected vertices.

\textbf{Discussion.} These results highlight the importance of \textit{update locality} (Section~\ref{sec:motivation}). Disabling any design introduces unrelated adjacency movement, full descriptor publication, or graph-wide scans. Dominated by these overheads, the variants' execution times increase by only 1.08$\times$--1.44$\times$ as batch size grows from 1K to 100K, whereas CoIncGraph's cost scales with the affected region. The three designs jointly preserve update locality across adjacency maintenance, topology publication, and incremental computation.

\subsection{Communication Analysis}
\label{sec:communication}

We further quantify the CPU--GPU communication of CoIncGraph and Grapin, both of which keep graph topology in host memory. During the processing of update batches, we sample the PCIe counters of the GPU through NVML, which capture all host-to-device (H2D) and device-to-host (D2H) traffic, including zero-copy accesses, and separately record the bytes of explicit \texttt{cudaMemcpy} transfers.

\textbf{Exp8: Communication Volume Reduction.} As shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:optimization-communication}(b), CoIncGraph reduces the per-batch traffic by 37.6$\times$ to 1495.6$\times$. Grapin transfers 9.8--10.5,GB in every batch regardless of the batch size, due to explicit descriptor reloads of all vertices (Table~\ref{tab:motivation}) and extensive zero-copy adjacency accesses caused by reactivating all vertices. CoIncGraph transfers only 6.6--278.8\,MB per batch, since its explicit transfers are limited to the descriptors of changed sources and the incoming adjacency of affected vertices (25\,KB--3.0\,MB), and only vertices whose results improve read their adjacency. As its traffic grows with the affected region, the reduction narrows to 37.6$\times$--178.6$\times$ with 100K updates per batch, consistent with the narrowing speedups in Table~\ref{tab:overall}.

\textbf{Exp9: Varying the ratio of insertions and deletions.} We vary the insertion/deletion ratio of each batch from 0/100 to 100/0 on TW with ten batches of 100K edge updates, constructing the workloads as described above. As shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:sensitivity}(a), CoIncGraph outperforms Grapin and Ingress for SSSP, BFS, and CC under all ratios, with speedups of up to 5.66$\times$ and 391.5$\times$, respectively. The execution time of Grapin is almost insensitive to the ratio, since it reactivates all vertices in every batch regardless of the batch composition. In contrast, CoIncGraph runs fastest in the insertion-only workload, where no result is invalidated and computation propagates only from the improved destinations; once deletions are present, deletion repair adds a nearly constant cost per batch, which is more pronounced for CC because deleting a dependency edge invalidates all vertices inheriting the component label through it. For PR, an edge update perturbs the contributions to all out-neighbors of its source, which spread over a large part of the graph, so CoIncGraph performs comparably to Grapin (0.80$\times$--1.11$\times$) while remaining 3.7$\times$--4.2$\times$ faster than Ingress.

\textbf{Exp10: Varying the number of changed sources.} Since CoIncGraph maintains graph views at the granularity of changed sources (Section~\ref{sec:view-maintenance}), its maintenance cost depends not only on the batch size but also on how the updates are distributed over sources. To isolate this factor, we fix each batch to 100K edge updates (half insertions and half deletions) on WK and vary the number of changed sources per batch from 5K to 100K, so that each changed source receives 20 to 1 updates. The sources are sampled across degree ranges from vertices with at least 20 out-neighbors, and we run BFS over ten batches. As shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:sensitivity}(b), CoIncGraph outperforms Grapin and Ingress under all settings, with speedups of 4.95$\times$--6.57$\times$ and 262.0$\times$--304.2$\times$, respectively. Although the changed sources increase by 20$\times$, the execution time of CoIncGraph increases only from 50.0\,ms to 84.3\,ms per batch (1.69$\times$). In contrast, Grapin is consistently slower (328.6--417.7\,ms per batch), as its cost remains dominated by graph-wide descriptor reloading and vertex reactivation (Section~\ref{sec:motivation}), which are independent of how updates are distributed. The speedup over Grapin therefore narrows from 6.57$\times$ to 4.95$\times$ as updates spread over more sources, but CoIncGraph retains its advantage even when every update touches a distinct source, which is the worst case for source-granular maintenance.
